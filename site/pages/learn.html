What is dissociation?

Dissociation is a disconnection between things that are usually connected: thoughts, memories, feelings, actions, surroundings or sense of self. It happens on a spectrum. Mild, everyday dissociation is common, like driving somewhere and not remembering the journey, or getting lost in a daydream.

Dissociation can also be a way the mind protects itself during overwhelming experiences. When the threat has passed, that protection can keep switching on, sometimes in response to reminders (triggers) that aren't obviously connected to the past.

Some ways people describe it:

  • Feeling far away, foggy, numb or "behind glass"
  • The world or your own body feeling unreal (derealization, depersonalization)
  • Gaps in memory, or "lost time"
  • Finding things you don't remember buying or writing
  • Feeling like a different age, or like someone else is in control

Dissociative identity disorder (DID)

DID is a recognized mental health condition in which a person's identity is divided between two or more distinct identity states, often called parts, alters, headmates or selves, alongside recurring gaps in memory. Each part may have its own way of thinking, feeling, speaking or relating to the world. Together they make up one person, sometimes called a system.

DID is understood to develop in early childhood as a response to severe, repeated trauma, often when a child has no way to escape and little protective care. It's a creative survival response, not a character flaw.

Some words people with DID use, which iDID also understands:

  • Fronting: the part (or parts) currently present and in control.
  • Co-fronting / co-consciousness: more than one part present or aware at the same time.
  • Switching: a change in which part is fronting.
  • Littles, protectors, caretakers: common roles parts can have.

Related conditions include other specified dissociative disorder (OSDD), dissociative amnesia, and depersonalization/derealization disorder. With the right support, usually long-term, trauma-informed therapy, people with dissociative disorders can and do get better, with more communication, cooperation and safety within the system.

Common myths

MythWhat's true
DID is the same as schizophrenia, or "split personality".They're different conditions. DID involves divided identity and memory gaps, not psychosis.
People with DID are dangerous.People with dissociative disorders are far more likely to be hurt by others, or to struggle with self-harm, than to hurt anyone.
DID is always obvious and dramatic.Most of the time it's hidden. Many people go years without a diagnosis because switches can be subtle.
It's made up for attention.DID is recognized in the standard diagnostic manuals and is closely linked to childhood trauma. Many people work hard to hide it.
Nothing helps.Specialized, phase-based trauma therapy helps many people live fuller, safer lives.

Trauma responses

When the body senses danger, it reacts automatically, often faster than thought. Common responses include:

  • Fight: anger, irritability, wanting to push back.
  • Flight: restlessness, panic, wanting to escape.
  • Freeze: going still, numb or blank; dissociation often lives here.
  • Fawn: placating, people-pleasing, going along with others to stay safe.

These responses made sense when they first developed. They can still switch on when you're safe, in response to a smell, a sound, a date on the calendar, raised voices or feeling trapped. Noticing what comes before a response is often the first step to softening it.

Grounding: coming back to now

Grounding helps your mind and body reconnect with the present moment. Try whatever feels okay; skip anything that doesn't.

  • 5-4-3-2-1: name 5 things you can see, 4 you can feel, 3 you can hear, 2 you can smell, and 1 you can taste.
  • Slow breathing: in for about 4, out for about 5 or 6. A longer out-breath tells the body it's safe to settle.
  • Box breathing: in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4, hold for 4, like tracing the sides of a square. The steady count gives a busy mind something simple to follow.
  • Orientation: say today's date, where you are, and one thing that shows it's now, not then.
  • Temperature: cool water on your hands or face, or holding something warm.
  • Movement: press your feet into the floor, stretch, or walk slowly while noticing each step.
  • Fresh air and daylight: step outside or open a window for a few minutes.

Supporting someone you love

  • Believe them. Being believed is protective. You don't need to understand everything to be supportive.
  • Stay calm and kind if they seem far away, younger, or unlike themselves. Speak gently, say who you are, and offer simple grounding: "You're safe. It's Tuesday. We're at home."
  • Respect every part without trying to make any of them go away. Use the names and pronouns the person shares.
  • Notice without judging. Your observations about what happened, when, and what helped can fill in missing memories, if they want that. iDID lets circle members add what they noticed.
  • Encourage professional support and help practically: lifts to appointments, reminders, patience.
  • Look after yourself, too. Caregivers need support. NAMI offers free family programs.

Getting help

If you or someone else is in immediate danger, call 911.

For any kind of emotional crisis in the US, call or text 988 or chat at 988lifeline.org, free, confidential and available 24/7. Prefer texting? Text HOME to 741741. Veterans: dial 988, then press 1.

Finding a therapist who understands dissociation

  • Look for clinicians with training in trauma and dissociation. The ISSTD directory lists members of the International Society for the Study of Trauma and Dissociation.
  • Psychology Today lets you filter therapists near you by "dissociative disorders" and "trauma and PTSD".
  • For lower-cost care, try community health centers (fees based on income) or Open Path Collective.
  • Questions worth asking: Do you have experience with dissociation? How do you approach safety and pacing? What happens if I feel overwhelmed in a session?

Why noticing and tracking can help

Memory gaps make it hard to see the whole picture. Writing things down, even a single word, can help you and your care team spot what tends to come before hard moments, which parts appear when, and which coping ideas actually help. That turns "it just happens" into something you can understand, plan around and talk about.

Go gently. Tracking should feel supportive, never like homework or surveillance. If it starts to feel heavy, take a break; iDID will still be there.
